Session 1 — The Ragged Edge of Space

Six came to the Thides system the way freight comes to anywhere, sealed in a crate, iced down, waiting for the motion to stop. His computer woke him when the crate did, and the first problem of his new life was that someone had shoved the crate against a wall over the bold “do not block” stencil on its face. He read that as a breach. He solved it like one, leaned into the door until it gave. Then he did the thing he was built to do and became someone else, a dock worker in chameleon livery, and walked past two bored guards who never once looked at what they were guarding.

He reached the security office before the men who arrived like people. On the way he catalogued every broken rule he passed, wrote them down with descriptions and last-known locations, and handed the list to Solveig Kaufer like a man returning lost property. It was not malice and it was not zeal. It was the doctrine. Finish what you start, clean, leave no loose ends. That the station ran on the exact opposite principle was information he had not yet processed.

Processing came later, at a table in the Comet’s Tail, where Ronin Sanchez put a glass in his hand and told him to slow down. The drink went down like warmth, and Six likes warmth, because the cold is the one thing his body remembers hating from the rack. The lobster was an armored thing between him and the meat, so he took the armor off with his hands and ate like it was his last meal, because every meal is. And somewhere in the middle of it a joke landed and something came loose in him, and he heard himself say it out loud and much too loud. “This is fun. This is FUN. I AM HAVING FUN.” Nobody had ever installed a volume knob for joy. He did not have one yet.

The weeks after taught him the shape of the place, and he learned it the only way he knows, by testing it. He wrote tickets for everything, every infraction, every night, and watched which charges turned into pay and which ones simply vanished. The station had a machine for justice and the machine was a liar. It ate the charges against the connected and kept the ones against the poor. Six is not built to be angry about that. He is built to notice it, file it, and wait to be told what the objective is. Nobody has told him yet.

Session 2 — The MacMillian Anomaly

The one thing he wanted for himself, he wanted badly and out loud, and it went nowhere. He had decided he would fight in the illegal pits under Station 45, not for money he doesn’t understand and can’t hold anyway, but because a fight is the one grammar he speaks without an accent. So he leaned on the Nova Nexus staff to put him on the card, and a bored guard sent him to a door with an orange handle in Section 102, and the door opened onto a dead maintenance shaft and a station-wide alarm and nothing else. Rock Lavey and Shackleton Magellan worked out afterward that the man had simply lied to get rid of him. Six had not considered that a person would do that. He filed it. The gap between what someone says and what someone means is a country he keeps arriving in without papers.

Then the four-a.m. alarm dragged them all down into Processing II, a sector that was supposed to be sealed and wasn’t, and Six went first, because he always goes first, because that is the shape the vat pressed into him. When Zigurds Sloan’s torch caught the blast door the welds told a story he could read cold: cut and resealed, cut and resealed, someone in and out of a locked room and careful to hide it. And in the loading bay was a container that was two things at once. One wall was human wiring, racks and terminals, honest ugly work. The other was grown, not built, symbols crawling across a surface that was part of the wall the way a scar is part of skin. He has no manual for that. He has no manual for anything that is genuinely, entirely new, and this was the first thing in his short conscious life that answered to no file he carries. It should have stopped him. Instead the container started to move, sliding toward the dark, and Six jumped the widening gap without deciding to, because the objective was in the box and the box was leaving and you finish what you start.

Inside was work, and work is the one place he is fully himself. He named the relay for what it was, a bridge between the human console and the grown one, and he cut every cable joining them, red and blue and green, no time to sort which mattered so all of it mattered. The doctrine is clean and total: leave nothing joined, leave no loose end. Then the cylinders split and the ooze came out eating the electronics as it went, and the grown material on the walls began to knit itself shut where his blade opened it, healing like something alive, and he answered the only way he knows. He spun the vibro-axe in tight zero-G arcs and carved two chunks free before the ooze could reach them and kicked them clear into open space, evidence the box could not swallow. When the count hit zero and the light took the container he was already burning clear on his suit thrusters, and behind him the whole thing folded into what the station’s sensors would only call a vortex. He came out of it holding proof of a thing this whole quiet corner of space is built on pretending isn’t real.

The medics wanted to open him up and check what the ooze might have left behind, and he signed their waiver instead and kept his suit sealed, which is its own kind of loose end and he knows it, and made it anyway. Then he stood in the debrief and told Holger Graeme and Solveig Kaufer every piece of it flat and complete, the relay, the two terminals, the ooze, the healing metal, the timer, the vortex, because a witness leaves a clean report the way a breacher leaves a cleared room. The station manager put his head in, looked stressed, and left again to go tend to a rich man’s comfort, and Six watched the strangest thing anyone on this station had ever seen get filed under someone’s inconvenience. By morning the crew was reassigned to Thides III, shipped off aboard a 113A hull on a maintenance escort that Shackleton Magellan and Rock Lavey read for exactly what it was, a hand on the back of the neck steering them out the door. Six carried the same understanding he carried out of the container. He is the loose end now. Somebody built a very careful lie and he cut a hole in it, and the tidy thing to do with a loose end is to remove it, and he of all things knows precisely what that means.

Session 3 — Buckle Up, Motherfuckers

They put him on a ship with no armor and no guns and he said the true thing about it out loud, that it was incomplete, because a breaching hull without teeth is a body without a spine and he does not have the words to be polite about a fact. Jareth explained it away, weight and cargo and a sector where nothing shoots back, and Six filed the explanation next to the ship and waited to see which one the world would prove right. At every relay stop he was off the ramp before it touched, weapon up, the landing zone his before anyone told him it was a landing zone, because that is the shape the vat pressed into him and the shape does not care that this was supposed to be maintenance. The world proved him right somewhere over the eleventh station, when a blob of something took half an engine off clean and a second one started eating the rest, and he opened the ramp into the wind and did the one useful thing available: marked the source a mile southwest, logged the numbers, and rode the crash down into the dark with the rest of them.

On the platform he met the thing with no file again. A hexagon of grown metal with symbols crawling across it, wired into the comms, the same handwriting as the container that took him into the vortex — the tech that answers to nothing he carries. Last time it had been leaving and he chased it. This time it was standing still, jamming them, lying to the whole relay network in someone else’s name, and so he did the doctrine to it. He put the axe through the wiring and cut its power and the platform’s power with it, left nothing joined, left the swarm-beacon running as a decoy for the Chitinoteuthis because a loose end you can aim is a tool, and went down into the jungle to find where the handwriting came from. He found a compound of it, fifty people and two ships and the cartel woman from the gate watching the trees, and he did what he was built to do. He became someone else — the suit went to shimmer and he went to nothing — and he walked into their fence the way he once walked past two guards on a dock.

Inside the older ship he read another cockpit full of the impossible, alien tech spliced into the nav, a jump logged straight down through an atmosphere that should have stopped it, and in the back a man wiring more of the grown bricks to wires that ran off the ship. Six took him the efficient way, a hand over the sound and an arm across the throat until the body went slack, no shot, no noise, a capture instead of a corpse because a quiet man is a smaller problem than a loud death right then — the math, not mercy. He tied him with the man’s own wire and opened the ramps and let the crew pour in, and when Ronin Sanchez lit the engines Six was already accounting the hold: a ship that jumps without a gate, a prisoner who breathes, a hundred pounds of the handwriting he cannot read. He came off Thides III holding the thing he could not hold before. Last time he cut a hole in the lie and kept only what he could carve loose in the seconds he had. This time he is flying the whole lie home in his hands.

Session 4 — The Case of the Missing Courtesan

The engines were nearly dry and somebody said so out loud, and Six offered to get out and push. Then he laughed, a hard mechanical bark with no warmth anywhere in it, and announced that he had made a joke. Nobody had asked him to try. He is learning the shapes of people the way he learned the shapes of a hull, by finding the seams and testing them, and a joke is a seam. When the station came up he did not wait for the docking ring to finish extending. He opened an airlock, put the thrusters in his hands, and crossed the vacuum to the airlock alone, because the ring takes minutes and the crossing takes seconds and there has never been a version of him that chooses the slower correct thing. He wrote the report on the way in, all of it, numbered by paragraph and subsection, everything the crew saw on Thides III set down once and set down properly.

The station handed him a woman on the promenade watching them eat. He knew her face off the broadcast channels before anyone else did, walked back out, and asked Pia Kadyrov flat why she was following him, then whether she was a hooker, then whether she had any blow. He was not being cruel. He was working through a list. Later, in her one room full of wire and screens, she said the words “your unknown origins” and his hand went to the axe before the sentence finished, because there is exactly one subject on which Six does not run the math first. She meant the alien tech. She had no idea how close she came to never explaining that. He put the hand down and asked her what “libel” means, and she told him, at length, and he listened.

The Marshal wanted him to say out loud what he had already written down. He refused her first, cited Holger Graeme’s standing orders and the station regulation clauses that back them, and made her go and fetch a better authority. When she came back with it he answered every question by reading her the paragraph number where the answer already was. Verbal verification of a written record is waste, and waste offends him more reliably than insult does. What he did not say, because Julija Borja never asked, is that the ship they impounded on her word was already gone by the time she was done being thorough.

Then the corridor on the cargo level, and a wire at knee height in bad light that nobody else had seen yet. Comms dead. Canisters rolling. Six sealed the suit, went to shimmer, and walked into the gas toward the people who had come to kill him, because cover is where you wait to be found and the fastest end to a fight is the far end of it. He put three rounds through a visor at twenty-five yards through smoke, into the one seam in the armour, and the man was dead before the decision finished. Not anger. Not mercy either. Just the shortest available line between a threat and no threat, which is the only doctrine he has ever run. They pulled a Kinetic Logistics ID Card off the body. The same people. On his station now, where he lives.
