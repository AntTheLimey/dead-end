Rock Lavey

View character sheet →

Session 1 — The Ragged Edge of Space

Rock Lavey woke first, which felt about right. Somebody has to be conscious to watch the others come up, and that job has fallen to him his whole life, in operating theatres and in worse rooms since. The docking ring smelled of recycled air and hard labor, and through the glass the bots crawled the hulls like they were dressing a wound that would never close. He had shipped himself here frozen to save the credits he did not have. A struck-off surgeon does not get to be proud about how he travels.

He is the sharp eye, and the eye did not stop working just because the license did. At the Silk Abbey he clocked the substances moving through the crowd before anyone said a word, the libido enhancers that were harmless enough and the amphetamine underneath them that was not, the kind of thing that turns a good night into a body on a slab at a high enough dose. Later, in the Nova Nexus holo-pit, he read the fight the way he reads everything, from the inside out. The veteran Clubber was being walked to an easy win before a bigger night. The setup was plain to anyone who knew where to look, and Rock always knows where to look.

What he carries and does not say is heavier than any of it. He scanned Six once and learned exactly what the big man is, property with a maker’s mark and a bounty on it, and he chose to say nothing. Two debtors on the same dead-end station, bound by a secret one of them does not even know is being kept. Rock has spent a career deciding who lives on a table. He decided this one quietly, the way he decides everything now, and moved on before anyone could thank him for it.

Session 2 — The MacMillian Anomaly

Weeks of patrols had taught Rock the station’s real anatomy, the way a body teaches you where it hurts. So when the work took him and Six down to Station 45 on the Main Line and a knot of gang men tried to fold themselves back into the shadows, he already knew what he was looking at before Six pointed them out. He did the talking. Not because he trusts men like that, but because talk buys distance, and distance is the only thing a non-combatant medic ever really has to spend. He kept his voice level and his hands where they could be seen, and he got what he came for, that Harsha Trent had welched on the bookies and been thrown out like refuse. Then he counted the perimeter closing around them, ten more drifting in loose, and he read the arithmetic of the room the way he reads a failing patient. Time to go. He and Six turned and walked, unhurried, before anyone forced the issue.

The evening went sideways in a smaller, stupider way, and Rock was the one who caught it. Six had chased a tip from a Nova Nexus guard straight into a dead corridor and a station-wide alarm, and afterward, sitting with Shackleton Magellan and running back the man’s face and posture in his head, Rock saw the whole con laid out clean. The guard had never known a thing. He had just wanted them gone, and he had picked the loud one and pointed him at nothing. Rock found it funny in the flat, tired way he finds most things funny now. People give themselves away in the smallest tells, a slackness around the mouth, a beat too long before the answer, and he has spent a lifetime watching for exactly that half-second when the body says what the mouth won’t.

The four a.m. alarm was the one that mattered. Before they geared up he went through his own supplies and pulled three stimulants, the kind he’d tell you flatly can stop a heart as easily as start one if you push them into the wrong body under stress, and he tucked them away anyway, because a man going into an offline sector with no cameras plans for the worst thing he can imagine. When the container in Processing II tore loose and the depressurization warnings lit every surface red, Rock did not freeze and did not play hero. He and Ronin Sanchez got into the break room and sealed the door, and he stayed there with a man he could actually protect while Six leapt into the dark after the thing that vanished. He came out the far side of it having witnessed something the whole Republic insists cannot exist, non-human tech and a vortex and the careful, deliberate work of someone who badly wanted it never found. He can prove none of it. He knows what he saw.

The orders to Thides III arrived the next morning dressed up as a maintenance escort, and Rock read the label off it in a heartbeat. You do not ship your only witnesses to an exclusion zone the same week a corporate money-man is in your entertainment district unless you want them somewhere they can’t talk. He said as much, quietly, to Shackleton, who had already reached the same floor. He is not angry about it the way Ronin Sanchez is angry. Anger is a luxury for people who still expect the powerful to play fair. Rock just notes it, the way he notes a pulse threadying under his fingers, and files it, and gets on the ship, because the crew is getting on the ship and he does not leave the crew.

Session 3 — Buckle Up, Motherfuckers

The crash sorted the crew into the conscious and the not, and once again Rock was the one still awake, which is the oldest arrangement of his life. Six, Ronin Sanchez, Jareth — all three folded and still in a smoking hull in a jungle that had already started sending things to eat them, and one medic on his feet with a hand of stimulants he keeps precisely because a man plans for the worst thing he can picture. He went down the line and brought them back one at a time, a shot and a slap and a name, the way you work a room full of the nearly-dead when there is no room and no light and the walls are dissolving under acid spit. Nobody thanks you for that either. You just do the next body.

He kept the eye working through all of it, because the eye is the only thing the license never took. When the rolling furred things came for the wreck he put the omni rifle on stun out of habit and instead of dropping the creature it began to purr, delighted, rolling in the discharge like a cat in sun, and Rock filed that under the flat exhausted comedy that is most of what he finds funny now — a whole planet built to kill you and here was a piece of it enjoying the wrong end of his gun. Later, aboard the ship they stole, it was Rock who traced the bricks in the back to the wires running off the hull and understood the shape of it a half-second before it mattered: the whole hold was rigged, and the only reason they were not a cloud of debris was that Six had clocked the same wires on his way in. Two men who read the room from opposite ends, arriving at the same answer in time.

He shed the heavy surgical case in the jungle to keep pace, which cost him something he will not say out loud — a surgeon setting down his tools to run is a surgeon admitting there are nights the running matters more. But he ran, and the crew lived, and this time they did not come out of it empty. A ship that jumps without a gate, a bound man breathing in the back, a hold full of the impossible. Last time all he had was the thing he’d said to himself in the dark, that he could prove none of it and knew what he saw. Tonight the proof is bolted to the deck under his boots. And the trouble with proof, he knows the way he knows a threadying pulse, is that the people it damns will want it back.

Session 4 — The Case of the Missing Courtesan

He spent the flight home with his own stitches and a medical kit, which is the closest thing to rest he gets. When the Marshal’s voice came through the cockpit demanding to know who they were, it was Rock who found the line that worked, MacMillian security on authorised orders, said flat and bored and exactly the way a man says a thing he has said a hundred times. Twenty years of talking to people who could take everything from him taught him that the trick is never the lie. The trick is sounding like the truth is too dull to bother decorating.

Then the station, and steak and lobster and a woman on the promenade watching them from a distance that Rock clocked before she wanted him to. And then a missing woman, which is a different kind of work, and the kind he is quietly better at than anyone else on the crew. At The Silk Abbey he asked for the women one at a time, in private, and that choice is the whole of him. He could have stood them in a room and asked questions at all of them. Instead he gave each one a closed door and his full attention, the way you take a history off a frightened patient, and they told him things nobody was going to volunteer to a uniform. McKenna told him about the party. About Brianna Melvin being asked to stay behind. About the trips to Aethel and the gifts, once or twice a month, routine, until this time.

In Room 2002 he worked the left bedroom with Shackleton Magellan and found what a doctor finds. Ammonia, hard and fresh in a room that had no business smelling of anything. No bedding. No marks. A space cleaned by people who knew what they were cleaning it of, which is a smell he can read as easily as a chart. And behind the headboard, missed, one earring. He has stood in enough rooms where the evidence was already gone to know precisely what that room was, and precisely how little that knowledge is worth to anyone with the power to act on it. Brianna Melvin has a child and a mother over in Processing I. Nobody in that hotel was ever going to be asked about either.

When the gas came down the corridor on the cargo level he moved to engage, which he does now without the pause he used to have. Struck off, bankrupt, and running, and he still walks toward the shooting. The eye is the thing the licence never took, and so is that.
