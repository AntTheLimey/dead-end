Ronin Sanchez

View character sheet →

Session 1 — The Ragged Edge of Space

Ronin Sanchez arrived late and unbothered, which is its own kind of resume. He came through the gate after the others, declared every weapon on him rather than gamble on a scan finding them first, and walked into the middle of Holger Graeme’s briefing with the easy confidence of a man who has been late to funerals. Four arms, two guns, and a body that technically belongs to the management that dismissed him. He does not advertise that last part. The repo-men already know where to look.

He is the crew’s social engine, and the night out was his doing. Ninety percent chance of a good time, he announced, like a man reading odds off a board, and then he set about making the number true. He took Six in hand and taught him to drink, told him to slow down and sip, and watched the big property-grown killer discover warmth and lobster and, God help everyone in earshot, fun. There was something close to pride in it. Ronin has spent his life guarding other people’s bodies. Guarding this one through its first good night was almost a pleasure.

He is not subtle, and it cost the crew once. When Shackleton Magellan was three seconds from lifting Leocadia Zavia’s coordinates through a hijacked bot, it was Ronin who shouted the bot away, blind to the operation running right past his elbow. He bet the underdog kid in the Nova Nexus pit too, on principle or on hope, and the veteran put the kid down in the third. Ronin cashed out ahead anyway, because the house wanted the new security to win. He took the money. A man in his position does not turn down a rigged win. He just files who rigged it, and why, for later.

Session 2 — The MacMillian Anomaly

Ronin ran the odds on the break room the second the depressurization warnings lit, and the odds said seal it. He got Rock Lavey inside and dogged the hatch with two hands while the other two braced, because that is the whole job: put a wall between the people you’re supposed to keep alive and the vacuum that wants them. He watched through the port as Six did the exact opposite, threw himself across the widening gap into a container already drifting for open space, and Ronin filed it the way he files everything reckless. Low survival probability, high entertainment value. The kid made it. He usually does, and one day he won’t, and Ronin will have called it and nobody will have listened, same as always.

He had spent the day being the wrong man for the room. Stood post in the entertainment sector next to Shackleton Magellan, watched the manager’s assistant Solveig Kaufer take notes on the visiting investor like she was building a case, and kept his mouth shut because saying the useful thing was Shack’s department, not his. Then the container. Then the ooze, the symbols nobody could read, the flash of light and the thing the station’s own sensors called a vortex. Ronin has guarded bodies his whole life, and he knows what it looks like when powerful people decide a thing did not happen. It looked exactly like this. Someone had welded that door shut and cut it open and welded it again, over and over, careful as a surgeon, and then the manager walked out of the debriefing to go pour drinks for a rich man. That told him more than the alien metal ever could.

Then they handed him the stick, which was the one clean thing in the whole mess. Best pilot on the station, and for once somebody in the building had the sense to say it out loud: he would fly them down to Thides III in the 113A, and he would fly them well, and he told them their odds on that count were excellent because they were. And then they told him why, and the good feeling went out like a match. This was not a posting. It was a broom. Sweep the four nobodies off the deck while the money is still aboard, park them somewhere restricted and quiet where whatever they saw stops mattering. His temper came up hot and fast the way it always does, and he held it, because a four-armed man with empty pockets learns early that swinging is a luxury he cannot afford. He kept quiet, which for Ronin is its own kind of violence. He poured the mexican whisky instead, told the crew flat that he did not trust the canteen either and that their odds of ever proving what they saw sat somewhere south of nothing. He would still fly them. That part never changes.

Session 3 — Buckle Up, Motherfuckers

They finally handed him the stick for real, and the first thing he did with it was blow the odds. He went to hot-start the engines inside the hangar the way an angry man does a thing, and the launch protocol he skipped nearly put the ship through a station wall before Jareth got on the radio and saved him from himself. Ronin filed his own mistake the way he files everyone else’s — noted, no excuses — and did not make a second one. When the blob took the engines over Thides III and turned a transport into a falling rock, he flew the rock. Threaded a dying hull through a jungle canopy and put it down hard enough to knock three of them cold and gentle enough that all three woke up, which is the only grade that matters. Low survival probability, and he beat it. For once the reckless number was his to beat and not Six’s.

The rest of the night was the job he actually knows, the one under the swagger. He walked point on a suicide odds and kept his mouth shut through a jungle full of things that hunt your heartbeat, and when the plan finally turned loud he was already moving. Over the fence on the smart rope, and a guard turning toward the noise found two pistols already up and Ronin already firing — a clean kill before the man could pull a breath to shout, because a bodyguard’s whole art is the half-second between a threat noticing you and a threat mattering, and Ronin has lived in that half-second his entire life. This is the thing people forget about the four-armed man pouring drinks and reading odds off a board. When violence is finally the affordable option, nobody in the crew does it faster or colder.

Then he dropped into the pilot’s seat of a stolen ship with alien engines and a compound full of angry men shooting at the hull, keyed the intercom, and told the crew to buckle up in the plainest terms available. He flew it out through dead heavy weapons and small-arms fire that couldn’t bite, threw the thing into evasion when the dissolving blobs came up off the surface after them, and cleared the satellite net on Jareth’s codes into open black. [Last time](…/…/chronicle/Sessions/Session 02/session-02-the-macmillian-anomaly.html) he’d told the crew flat that their odds of ever proving what they saw sat somewhere south of nothing. Tonight he was flying the proof — a gateless jump drive, a hold of alien tech, a bound man in the back — and for the first time since he took this dead-end job, the odds on that crew getting to the truth ticked up off the floor. He’d still bet against them. He just wouldn’t bet as much.

Session 4 — The Case of the Missing Courtesan

He brought her in clean. Reverse thrusters on the approach, deceleration worked properly against a fuel state with no margin in it at all, station ROVs on the hull and the ship set down where it was told to be set down. Then he powered her out and sat in the quiet cleaning his firearms, which is what he does with his hands when there is nothing to shoot, and watched Six decide that a docking ring was optional and go out the airlock into vacuum. He did not comment. There is a version of the job where you argue with that, and he has never worked it.

The rest of it he missed. Twenty-four hours of paid rest and relaxation put in front of a man who has spent a long time not being able to afford either, and he ate his way through it and then some, and spent the working half of the next day too sick to be any use to anybody. So while the crew were pulling hotel footage and reading a scrubbed room and taking fire on the cargo level, Ronin was flat somewhere with a gut full of the best meal he has had in years, missing the whole thing. A bodyguard is only worth what he is standing next to. He was not standing next to anyone.
