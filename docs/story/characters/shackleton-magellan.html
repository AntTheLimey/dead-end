Shackleton Magellan

View character sheet →

Session 1 — The Ragged Edge of Space

Shackleton Magellan reads a room like a system diagram, looking for the input nobody is watching and the output nobody has secured. He came up out of cryo beside Rock Lavey with the same cargo-class frost on him as everyone else, another broke man at the end of the lanes, except Shack is broke the specific way that comes from owing Corvid Financial more than a life is worth and skipping the system before they could collect.

He got to work the moment the tour ended. He cloned his company card onto his wrist computer, because a physical key is a leash, and he hacked the barracks environmental controls and locked the temperature at a civilized seventy-two, quietly overwriting the eighty degrees Six had set. It was the first negotiation the crew ever ran and nobody even noticed it happen. At the Comet’s Tail he steered a service bot across the floor and turned it into a microphone aimed at Leocadia Zavia, and he had her coordinates almost in hand when Ronin Sanchez bellowed at the bot to clear off and the whole thing died at the worst possible second. That is the job. You do everything right and someone loud undoes it for free.

He sees more than he says. The Nova Nexus roulette wheel was rigged in the crew’s favor, a clumsy little mechanism under the felt throwing the ball to black, and Shack watched it, understood it, and let it ride without a word. He would rather talk quantum physics with a hostess than accept the comp she was sent to offer, because underneath the debt and the running there is still the man who went out past the charted lanes to find something, and who came back owing everything except the belief that the finding was real. The dead-end system is exactly the kind of nothing that hides a something. He can feel it in the dark the void is wrapped around.

Session 2 — The MacMillian Anomaly

Shack has spent enough weeks on MacMillian Station to read its paperwork the way he reads a dead relay, hunting the one wire that carries current when nothing is supposed to be on. So when the morning board came up with their crew stamped as the TAC team and half the other slots left blank, he clocked it, the same way he’d clocked the clumsy little mechanism under the Nova Nexus roulette wheel his first week. The station’s commission system for arrests was rigged, someone behind the felt throwing the ball where they wanted it. He didn’t say much. He filed it, the way he files everything, into the running log he cannot stop keeping.

He and Ronin Sanchez drew the entertainment district, which is how he ended up watching Peter Goodwin hold court at The Silk Abbey with the station manager fawning and expensive company on both arms. The investor was the loud thing, the thing you were meant to look at. The interesting thing was Solveig Kaufer sitting a careful distance off, tapping notes into a tablet with far more detail than any assistant needs to remember a dinner. Shack has spent a whole career telling the decoy from the payload. Kaufer was writing something down, and whoever it was for, it was not the manager.

Then the four a.m. alarm dragged them into Processing II, and he got the night he has been chasing since before Corvid ever owned his name. The blast door came apart under Zigurds Sloan’s torch and the welds underneath were old, resealed and disguised over and over, a locked room somebody had been walking in and out of for a long time and taking real pains to hide it. He found the footprints himself, a single clean track through the dust from the stairwell to one sealed container out of a whole bay of them, and he worked the control room off Sloan’s password to find three of the four cargo slots empty and the fourth holding the crate. What was inside is the thing he will re-read that log for the rest of his life. Human comms gear bolted into racks on one wall, and on the other, technology grown into the metal like it had once been alive, symbols on it that no human being was ever supposed to read. That is the find. The great one, the one out past the charted lanes he always swore was real, and it was sitting in a mothballed cargo bay under a shipping label.

And he watched it get taken. When the container tore loose and the depressurization alarms went, Shack held the control room while Six jumped the gap, and the external cameras told him the rest of it, that every station drone was dark, killed remote by a hand that knew exactly which switch to throw. The crate went out into the black and a vortex swallowed it whole. He got a scan of the two chunks Six carved free, a bare surface read and no more, before station security bagged them for evidence and shipped them off to Sloan. Then the orders came down: escort a maintenance run to Thides III, an exclusion zone, off-station, out of the way, and he and Rock Lavey read it for exactly what it was. You do not reassign the only people who saw the thing unless you want them somewhere they cannot say so. He cannot prove a word of it. And yet he knows what he saw, and someone spent a great deal of effort making sure nobody ever would.

Session 3 — Buckle Up, Motherfuckers

They made him squadron leader for the run, which he took the way he takes most authority, as a set of variables he was now responsible for. He led it well enough right up to the point where the physics stopped cooperating: a hull with no armor, a substance that ate their engines out of a clear sky, and a crash that put three of the four of them under. When they picked themselves up in the jungle it was Shack who called the retreat off the wreck, because a survey man knows the difference between a position you can hold and a position that is already lost, and the crash site was a beacon screaming into a world that hunts by exactly that. So he moved them, toward the one relay left unserviced, and walked them straight into the seam he has been reading toward his whole career.

Relay Station 11 was a forgery. He read it the way he read the rigged commission board and the roulette wheel before it — the one wire carrying current that nothing was supposed to be on. Two years of maintenance logged clean under a name nobody knew, a hexagon of grown alien metal wired into the comms and lying to the whole network in that stranger’s hand. And when he put the telescope to the dark five miles off, there it was, the payload the whole restricted planet was the decoy for: a lit compound, ships on the pads, and a hyperspace vortex tearing open right against the atmosphere to drop a hull straight in without so much as brushing a satellite. That is the find. Not the container this time, gone into the black before he could hold it — a whole operation, standing still, close enough to walk to. The great one he swore was real out past the charted lanes, and it was running freight on a world he’d been told was empty.

So the treasure-hunter did the thing he almost never gets to do, which is take the treasure. He ran the numbers on Six’s EMP — blast radius here, spare the old ship, kill the command house and the Kinetic Logistics hull and the fences all in one pulse — and the numbers were his to run because for once nobody was shouting over the operation while it happened. It worked the way clean math works. They came over the fence into an EMP-dark compound, took the older ship whole, and Shack walked up the ramp of a vessel wired with alien nav and gateless jump drives and understood that he was no longer a man who had seen the impossible and lost it. He was a man carrying a piece of it home in a stolen hull, a running log finally attached to a thing he could set on a table. He has wanted proof his whole broke, running life. He has it now, and he is clear-eyed enough to know it is the most dangerous cargo he has ever flown.

Session 4 — The Case of the Missing Courtesan

He spent one of his gizmos on a tracker and put it on the ship’s transponder relay, quiet, no ping, answering only to his own frequency. Then he spent the rest of the three hours on the thing in the nav computer, and worked out what it was. Beaconless. Coordinates read straight off gravity wells instead of off the Republic’s markers, and a jump you could plot into the top of an atmosphere without dying of it. Nothing the Federal Republican Interseller Fleet has ever flown does that. He had the whole of it, the device and the drives and the wires running aft to something he never got to open, sitting under his hands for three hours. It is the find of his career and of anyone’s career, and he had a shift and a half with it before the Marshal’s people put a seal on the door.

So he did what he always does when the thing itself is taken away, which is build a record. He talked Holger Graeme into full station security access and pulled the Royale Hotel footage himself. He watched a woman go into a room and not come out. He watched Solveig Kaufer arrive and leave with two men, and a human cleaning crew go in where the droids should have gone, and he recognised one of their faces from a scan he ran months ago and never expected to need, Hildebrand Panza, military record redacted. He clocked the cameras going dark ten minutes early and coming back an hour late. In the suite he matched an earring to a photograph from memory. Down in the basement he noticed that the sheets going through the laundry were not the sheets from that room, which is the smallest observation of the session and possibly the most damning, because it means the bedding never went into the system at all.

He traded with the reporter because that is what you do when you cannot hold the evidence yourself. Give Pia Kadyrov the footage and the name, take her research and her reach, and put a copy of the truth somewhere outside the reach of the people who keep deleting it. He read Samina Ventura correctly and without flattering himself about it, an employer counting a lost asset and pricing a lawsuit, not a woman in grief. And when Julija Borja asked him questions he answered them, all of them, because a man who has spent years being disbelieved by institutions does not pass up the one institution finally taking notes.

Then he pinged the tracker, and nothing came back. He built the thing knowing its limit, that distance eats the answer, and so silence has exactly one meaning. The ship is out of the Thides System. It went out of a federal quarantine, with the alien nav and the gateless drives and the hold full of the impossible, and the only reason anyone on this station knows it happened is a gizmo he spent on a hunch. He had the proof in his hands for a day. He is a man who has now lost the great find twice, and the second time he watched it go from the inside of the building that was supposed to be holding it.
